# Writing like a person

The positive side of the catalogue. AI patterns come from choosing the statistically safest option. Human writing comes from choosing for one reader, one subject and one moment, so it is specific, uneven and committed. Use this when drafting fresh text and when finishing a rewrite. The numbers for Ayaahn's own voice are in `voice.md`; where they disagree with anything here, his samples win.

## The five habits

1. **Say the specific thing.** Replace the general claim with the detail only this subject has: a name, a number, a place, a small odd fact. If you can't think of one, the sentence probably isn't needed.
2. **Commit.** Take a position and state it plainly. Qualify only where the doubt is real, once, where it applies. Real hedges (*perhaps*, *tends to*, "I'm not sure") are human; piled hedges are not.
3. **Write for one reader.** Know who it is, what they already know, and what they need to decide. Lead with the point. Skip background they already have.
4. **Earn every sentence.** Each sentence adds a fact, a step in the argument, a reaction or a turn. If it only repeats, announces or praises, cut it.
5. **Let the shape follow the content.** Three items when there are three. A list only when the content is a list. A short paragraph when the thought is short.

## Rhythm and sentences

- Vary length by varying the thinking, not by chopping. Long sentences carry a reasoning chain; a short one lands a point. Ayaahn's essays average about 26 words a sentence with a wide spread (about 12), so expect long ones and let them breathe.
- Start sentences in different ways: with the subject, a clause, a time, an object. Use And, But, So at the start only where the speech rhythm calls for it (about 1 sentence in 20 in his samples).
- A comma splice or a run-on that sounds like him is fine. Don't tidy it into a template.
- Don't end every paragraph on a neat summary line. End on the last real point, or on the thing that turns the argument.
- Questions are rare in his writing. Ask one only when the reader would genuinely ask it.

## Word choice

- Plain verbs. *Is*, *has*, *makes*, *shows*, *uses*. Prefer the short word unless the long one is more exact.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions: "the school ran out of chairs" over "resource constraints emerged".
- One name for one thing. Repeat the noun instead of cycling through synonyms.
- Words belong to a register. Academic text stays formal with no contractions and no "I". Speeches and personal pieces are first person and use contractions. Essays sit between.
- Keep the writer's own words, even ones on the AI lists, when he really uses them ("at the end of the day", "to conclude", "utilize", "showcases" appear in his samples).
- British spelling, curly quotes, spaced em dashes, three plain dots for an ellipsis: Ayaahn's habits, so keep them.

## Tone and voice

- Match the occasion: a speech is spoken, an assignment is argued, a message is quick. Don't carry the speech voice into the essay.
- A person has opinions, mixed feelings and tension they haven't resolved. Where the original has them, keep them: "I think this is mostly right, but something about it bothers me." Do not invent them where it didn't.
- Humour, asides and self-corrections are fine when they were there. A genuine aside in brackets beats a staged "honestly?".
- Specific, dated, local references (slang, a street, a teacher, a match) are strong human signals. Keep every one the source gives; never fabricate one.
- Be warm without flattering. Thank or praise only when something specific earned it.
- Admit limits plainly: "I don't know", "the source doesn't say". Don't fill the gap with a plausible guess.

## Structure

- **Open** with the point, a scene, a claim or a question the reader already has. Not with the topic's importance or a definition of the topic.
- **Develop** with the evidence in the order the argument needs. Each paragraph does one job. Paragraphs differ in length.
- **Close** on the last concrete fact, the consequence, or the turn. If the piece needs a conclusion, make it add something (a decision, an implication) rather than restating.
- Headings only in long documents, in sentence case, naming what the section holds.
- Prose over bullets for reasoning. Bullets for parallel items that really are parallel. No bold labels on each.
- In a reply to a person, answer first, then only the reasoning that would change their mind.

## A drafting routine

1. **Know the reader and the point.** Write the point in one plain sentence before anything else.
2. **Gather the specifics.** Names, numbers, examples, quotes from the user. Never invent any; ask for what's missing or write a simpler sentence.
3. **Draft fast, in plain words**, in the right register for the kind of piece (academic, essay, speech, personal).
4. **Cut:** every sentence that only announces, repeats, praises or hedges. Every opener that doesn't add anything.
5. **Check against `ai-patterns.md`.** Look at the large shapes first (contrast pairs, triads, closers, an inflated opening, a tidy ending), then the words.
6. **Read it aloud.** Where you stumble or sound like a brochure, rewrite the sentence around its main point.
7. **Run the script** (`scripts/run.py`) when the text is long enough, then re-read the output; the script removes patterns but cannot make dull writing good.
8. **Final check against the source:** no fact, name, number, quote or claim added or lost. Meaning, argument and opinion unchanged.

## Before and after

| AI-sounding | Human version | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| It's not just a school; it's a community that shapes who you become. | The school shaped me, and I'd say so on a bad day too. | State the claim; drop the staged contrast. Use only what the writer actually said. |
| The festival showcases a diverse array of cultural traditions, fostering unity. | The festival has [the actual stalls, dances or foods the source names]. | Replace the vague praise with the named detail. If none was given, ask for it or write a plainer sentence. |
| In conclusion, it is crucial to note that technology plays a pivotal role in education. | Technology is already in the classroom, whether teachers asked for it or not. | Cut the announced closer and the inflated verb; keep the one idea. |
| Studies show that sleep matters. | [Name the study], or "I've read that sleep matters" if it is the writer's own claim. | Vague attribution needs a real source or an honest first-person frame. |
| Despite these challenges, the town continues to thrive. | [The real problem] is the main issue, and [the real response, if any]. | Name the problem and the response, or cut the sentence. |

## Limits

No rule list makes dull writing good, and no tool can promise to beat a given AI detector. People who judge by feel do little better than chance, and human writing keeps absorbing AI habits, so several tells together matter more than any single one. The goal is simple: text that reads like Ayaahn wrote it for a real reader, says only what he meant, and is specific enough to be checked.
