# AI writing patterns to avoid

A catalogue of the tells collected from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup), the blader/humanizer and avoid-ai-writing skill catalogues, and the research on excess vocabulary and stylometry. Use it two ways: to avoid these habits when writing fresh text, and to check a draft after the script runs.

How to read it. A tell counts in proportion to how rarely a careful human would choose it on purpose. Strong tells justify an edit on one sighting. Weak tells (marked *weak*) need company from other tells in the same passage. Every pattern is a default: the choice that fits the widest range of readers and topics. A person writes for one reader and one subject, so their choices are uneven and specific.

## 1. Content: the root cause

**Regression to the mean.** The model replaces specific, odd, true facts with generic, positive ones. "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry". Fix: keep the specific fact, drop the praise.

**Significance inflation.** Ordinary facts are dressed as turning points.
- stands as a testament, plays a pivotal/crucial/vital role, marks a turning point, indelible mark, reflects broader trends, deeply rooted, enduring legacy, setting the stage for.
- Appears at three scales: a phrase, a stock "Challenges and Legacy / Future Outlook" section, a send-off paragraph.
- Nature and species writing: ecosystem importance and conservation talk with no real source.

**The "Despite..." formula.** "Despite its prosperity, X faces challenges... Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive." Name the real problem and the real response, or cut.

**Notability padding.** Listing outlets that mentioned someone ("featured in X, Y and Z"), "independent coverage", "active social media presence", follower counts. Say what was said, by whom, or cut.

**Superficial -ing analysis.** A participle tacked on to a fact: "...highlighting its importance", "...showcasing", "...underscoring", "...reflecting", "...fostering". Strongest when the subject is a thing, since a fact can't highlight anything.

**Promotional tone.** nestled, in the heart of, breathtaking, stunning, vibrant, rich cultural heritage, groundbreaking, must-visit, diverse array, boasts. State what the thing is.

**Vague attribution.** "Experts argue", "industry reports", "some critics", "studies show", "many believe". One or two sources inflated into a consensus. Name the source or cut the claim.

**Didactic disclaimers.** "It's important to note", "it is crucial to remember", "may vary", "always check". Say the thing.

**Summaries and conclusions.** An announced "In conclusion/Overall/In summary", a final paragraph that restates the opening, an hourglass shape (synthesis, body, synthesis). End on the last concrete fact.

**Borrowed depth.** Sayings that sound profound: "the real question is", "at its core", "what really matters", "X is the language of Y", "X becomes a trap".

**Knowledge-limit talk and guesses.** "As of my last update", "specific details are limited", "not widely documented", then a plausible guess ("likely grew up in a middle-class home"). State what the source doesn't show, or leave it out.

**Wrong reader.** In a reply, re-explaining background the other person already has, so the decision arrives last. Lead with the decision.

## 2. Language and grammar

**AI vocabulary** (weak alone, strong in clusters; lists change with every model release):
align with, bolster, bustling, crucial, delve (faded after 2025), enduring, enhance, ever-evolving, foster, garner, highlight (verb), indelible, interplay, intricate, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), leverage, meticulous, multifaceted, notably, nuanced, pivotal, realm, robust, seamless, shed light on, showcase, streamline, tapestry, testament, underpin, underscore, unpack, vibrant; also genuinely/keen as intensifiers, beacon, paradigm, cornerstone, burgeoning, quintessential, spearhead, synergy, actionable, impactful, learnings.
- The excess-word research found most of these are verbs (about two thirds) and adjectives, not topic nouns. They are style words, not subject words.
- Don't ban a word that is literal in context ("underscore" the character, "landscape" painting, "delve" in a mine).

**Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "marks", "represents", "boasts", "features", "offers" where "is" or "has" would do.

**Negative parallelism.** "Not just X, but Y", "It's not X, it's Y", "This doesn't mean X. It means Y", clipped tails ("no guessing"), chains ("No X. No Y. Just Z."). It names something nobody claimed so the real point sounds bigger. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader really holds.

**Rule of three.** Adjective, adjective, adjective; phrase, phrase, phrase; three examples then a lesson. Use three only when there are three things.

**False ranges.** "From the Big Bang to the cosmic web, from stars to dark matter": a "from... to..." with no real scale between the ends.

**Elegant variation.** The same person called the protagonist, the key player, the eponymous figure. Repeat the name.

**Stacked hedges.** "could potentially", "might possibly", "it's also possible that". Ordinary hedges (*perhaps*, *tends to*) are human; the pile-up is the tell.

**Transitions on rails.** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, That being said, In addition, Notably, Importantly. Often the connection can be shown by order, or by "and", "but", "so".

**Staging.** Let's dive in, here's what you need to know, here's the thing, the thing is, to be clear, don't get me wrong, honestly?, real talk, without further ado. The writer announces the point instead of making it.

**Arguing with nobody.** "This isn't about X", "I'm not saying Y", "you might think", "a tempting approach would be". An objection or option that appears nowhere else.

**Dramatic fragments and closers.** A one-line paragraph repeating the last one ("That's the real win."), "Read that again.", "Let that sink in.", a row of fragments, ALL-CAPS or every. single. word. Merge or cut.

**Narrated candor and performed insight.** "In the interest of full disclosure", "the line I keep coming back to", "I can't stop thinking about this", "what's interesting here is".

**Sycophancy and chatbot residue.** "Great question!", "You're absolutely right!", "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like me to...?", "Let me know if...", "Here is a...". The most certain tells.

**Repeated sentence openers.** Three or more sentences in a row starting with the same word, by rule, not by ear. (Repetition on purpose for rhythm is fine.)

**Passive and subjectless sentences** (*weak*). "No configuration needed. Results are preserved automatically."

**Hyphenated modifiers kept after the noun** (*weak*). "The report is high-quality."

## 3. Punctuation and formatting

- **Em dashes**: used as a universal connector, in a punched-up sales way, where a comma, colon or parentheses fits. *Weak alone*; many editors use dashes. Match the writer's own rate.
- **Bold as decoration**: bold on every key term, bold-label bullets ("**Performance:** ..."). Remove or turn into prose.
- **Inline-header vertical lists**: bullets that each start with a bold label and colon.
- **Title Case headings** ("Strategic Negotiations And Global Partnerships"). Sentence case, keeping real proper nouns.
- **Emoji** at the start of headings, bullets or lines. Arrows (→) as decoration.
- **Rules between every section**, a heading that repeats the document title, a heading repeated in the first sentence under it.
- **Curly quotes** (*weak*; Word and phones auto-curl). Keep them if the writer uses them.
- **"Subject:" lines** pasted into a message that isn't an email.
- **Uniform paragraph length** and even sentence rhythm: no three consecutive sentences of the same length is the most measurable signal. Wall-of-text replies with no line breaks are the opposite tell.
- **Excessive structure**: headings and bullets on a short answer that needed a paragraph.

## 4. Leftovers and artifacts (near certain)

- Chat markup that survived a paste: `citeturn0search0`, `iturn0image0`, `:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}`, `oai_citation`, `grok_card`, ↩ footnote marks, `<|endoftext|>`.
- `utm_source=chatgpt.com` or `utm_source=openai` on URLs.
- Unfilled placeholders: `[Name]`, `[Describe the issue]`, `2025-XX-XX`, `<!-- Add if available -->`.
- Refusals and "As an AI language model".
- Text that stops mid-sentence.
- Invented references, DOIs, ISBNs, categories; a reference list that doesn't match the text.
- A sudden switch of English variety (American defaults) or a sudden jump to flawless style.
- Reviewer notes or "submission statements" addressed to a reader who isn't there; summaries of the writer's own process ("anything unconfirmed is flagged rather than guessed").

## 5. Tells of a bad humanizer (avoid creating these)

- Staccato fragments chopped in to fake rhythm.
- Fake candor ("Honestly?", "Look,") and invented asides or opinions.
- Injected contractions or slang the writer never uses.
- Adding a fact, name, number, quote or citation. Subtract and sharpen; never invent.
- Cutting a real hedge, legal or safety notice, or a necessary caveat.
- Rewriting text that was already fine. If no justified edit remains, return it unchanged.

## 6. Not tells (don't act on these)

Perfect grammar; "bland" prose; fancy or academic words that aren't on the lists above; formal letters and salutations (these predate chatbots); conjunctions on their own; one em dash; curly quotes from a word processor; text written before 30 November 2022; a quotation, title or proper name containing a listed phrase.

## 7. About detectors

Perplexity and burstiness tools are unreliable: they flag formal, formulaic and non-native writing (in one study about 61% of non-native essays were flagged, against about 5% of native ones), and paraphrasing can defeat them. Wikipedia's own guidance says not to rely on them alone. Nothing here can promise to pass a given detector; aim for text that reads like a person wrote it for one reader.
